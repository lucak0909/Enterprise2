## Introduction

Most startups don't fail because the code was bad — they fail because the founders fell
out. When you start a company with classmates, everything feels equal and informal. That
is exactly the moment to write things down. A founders' agreement is a short, uncomfortable
conversation now that saves you a long, expensive one later. This section covers what to
agree on, how to split ownership fairly, and what protects you if a co-founder walks away.

## Key Concepts

**1. Equity split.** Who owns what percentage of the company. An equal split is simple and
signals trust, but it should reflect future contribution, not just who had the idea. Ideas
are cheap; four years of execution is not. Discuss time commitment, skills, and who is
going full-time first.

**2. Vesting and the cliff.** Founders earn their shares over time — typically **4 years
with a 1-year cliff**. Leave before 12 months and you keep nothing; after that, shares
vest monthly. *Example:* a co-founder with 30% who leaves at month 18 keeps 18/48 of
their shares (11.25% of the company), and the rest returns to the company.

**3. Roles and decision-making.** Agree who is CEO, who owns the product/tech, and how
deadlocks are broken. A 50/50 company with no tie-break rule can grind to a halt.

**4. IP assignment.** All code, designs, and inventions created for the company must be
legally owned by the company — not by individual founders. Investors check this first.
As ISE students, also check whether anything was built during placements or university
projects, since employers and the university may have IP policies that apply.

**5. Leaver provisions.** What happens to shares when someone leaves. A *good leaver*
(illness, mutual agreement) usually keeps vested shares; a *bad leaver* (misconduct,
joining a competitor) may have to sell them back at a low price.

## Case Study

**Snapchat (2011–2014).** In April 2011, Stanford student Reggie Brown pitched Evan
Spiegel the idea for a disappearing-photo app, and Spiegel brought in Bobby Murphy to code
it. Nothing about ownership was ever signed. Brown says there was a verbal deal; the others
denied it. Four months later Brown was locked out. He sued in 2013, claiming he was a
co-founder owed equity, and in 2014 Snapchat settled, crediting him with the original
idea. Snap's 2017 IPO filing later revealed the payout: **$157.5 million**.

*Lesson:* a one-page agreement signed at the start — covering equity, vesting and IP —
would have made ownership far clearer and likely avoided a long, costly lawsuit.

**Watch:** [How Much Equity to Give Your Cofounder — Michael Seibel, Y Combinator](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9NhEBVPlJs4)
(~9 min). Why near-equal splits are usually right, and why vesting with a cliff is your
main safety net if a co-founder leaves.

## Exercise

You and two ISE classmates are starting a company. One (the Builder) wrote the prototype,
one (the Connector) has industry contacts, and one (the Part-Timer) can only work part-time
until graduation.

1. Propose an equity split and justify it in two sentences.
2. Set a vesting schedule. What happens if the Part-Timer leaves at month 9? Month 24?
3. Who makes the final call on a disagreement about the product?
4. Who currently owns the prototype code — and how do you transfer it to the company?
5. Turn your answers into a one-page founders' agreement, adding what happens if someone
   leaves on bad terms. Then enter your draft into the **Founders' Agreement Stress Test**
   below and fast-forward 3 years. How many events did it survive, and which clauses would
   you change?

Compare your answers with a partner. Where did you disagree? That's the conversation to
have *before* you incorporate.

## Interactive Element (optional)

### Founders' Agreement Stress Test

The reader drafts an agreement for the Exercise trio (Builder, Connector, Part-Timer),
then fast-forwards 3 years to see if it holds up.

**1. Draft:** tap one option per clause.

- *Split:* 33/33/33 | 40/35/25 | 50/30/20 | custom
- *Vesting:* none | 4 yrs | 4 yrs + 1-yr cliff
- *Tie-break:* none | CEO decides | outside adviser
- *IP:* "sort it later" | assigned to company
- *Leaver terms:* none | good/bad leaver clause

**2. Events:** each shows PROTECTED or EXPOSED with a one-line reason.

- Month 9, the Part-Timer quits → needs a cliff (keeps 0%)
- Month 24, the Connector joins a competitor → needs a leaver clause
- The Builder and Connector deadlock on a pivot → needs a tie-break
- An investor asks who owns the code → needs IP assignment
- An ex-collaborator claims equity (the Reggie Brown event) → needs signed vesting + IP

**Vesting:** kept = 0 if month < 12, else equity × month / 48. Show ownership with the
cap-table bar from the Runway & Dilution Simulator (Finance and Valuation topic).

**Result:** X/5 survived, plus the clauses to fix. The split doesn't affect the score.
Ending line: *"Snapchat settled for $157.5M because none of this was signed."*
