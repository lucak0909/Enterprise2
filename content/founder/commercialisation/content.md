## Introduction

As engineers we're trained to ask "can we build it?" but as a founder the question that
actually matters is "will anyone pay for it?" Commercialisation is pretty much everything
between having a working prototype and having customers who keep paying you for it, and
in fairness it's where a lot of technically strong startups quietly die. When CB Insights
went through 101 startup post-mortems in 2014, the most common reason founders gave was
**no market need** (42%), which basically means they built something nobody wanted. Their
2021 update still had it near the top at 35%, just behind running out of cash. This section
covers how to test demand before you sink months into building, how to tell if you've
actually found product-market fit, and how to get your first few customers in the door.

## Key Concepts

**1. Problem before product.** Start with a problem that a specific group of people
already has and is annoyed by, not a cool bit of tech you're trying to find a use for.
The way you check this is with **customer discovery** interviews, and the best guide to
them is *The Mom Test*. The idea is simple enough. Talk about their life rather than your
idea, ask what they actually did the last time the problem came up rather than what they
*would* do, and listen a lot more than you talk. If someone says "that sounds great!"
they're just being nice to you, it isn't evidence.

**2. Minimum viable product (MVP).** Your MVP is the smallest thing you can make that
tests the assumption you're least sure about. It doesn't even need to be software. A
landing page, a spreadsheet or just doing the job by hand for your first few customers (a
"concierge" MVP) can prove people want something in a few days instead of a few months.

**3. Product-market fit (PMF).** This is when the market starts pulling the product out
of you, so people stick around, come back and tell their mates about it. A common way to
check is the **Sean Ellis test**, where you ask users how they'd feel if they couldn't use
your product anymore. If more than 40% say "very disappointed" that's a strong sign you're
there. Sign-ups are nice to look at but whether people actually stay is what matters.

**4. Business model and pricing.** You need to work out *who* pays and *how*, whether
that's a subscription, paying per use, a cut of each transaction or a licence. In B2B
the person **using** your product (a nurse, a developer) usually isn't the person
**buying** it (a manager with a budget), so you have to win over both. Price on the value
you're giving people rather than what it costs you to run, and start charging early since
someone actually handing over money is the one bit of validation you can't fake. (Whether
each sale actually makes you money is covered in Finance & Valuation.)

**5. Go-to-market (GTM).** This is just how you get the product in front of customers.
Pick a small **beachhead** market you can properly own first and expand out from there.
You also need to decide how you sell. **Self-serve** means a low price and lots of users
signing up online by themselves, while **sales-led** means a high price, a handful of big
customers, long sales cycles and a pile of procurement paperwork. Ireland is a small
market so it's worth planning to sell abroad from early on.

## Case Study

**Stripe (2010 to today).** Patrick and John Collison grew up in Dromineer in Co.
Tipperary and went to secondary school in Castletroy College in Limerick. Back in 2010,
taking payments online meant weeks of paperwork with banks and integrations that were a
pain to set up. Stripe picked a very specific beachhead, **developers**, and made
accepting payments a matter of a few lines of code.

Getting the first users was done completely by hand. Y Combinator calls what they did a
**"Collison installation"**. Most founders would say "we'll send you a link", but the
brothers would ask for the person's laptop and set Stripe up for them there and then. It
didn't scale at all, but it got people actually using Stripe and gave the brothers a
direct line for feedback. In 2025 alone, businesses on Stripe processed **$1.9 trillion**
in total volume.

*Contrast:* Quibi raised **$1.75 billion** before it launched in April 2020 and announced
it was shutting down about six months later because not enough people would pay for it.
Its own founders admitted the idea might not have been strong enough. They scaled up
before they'd checked that anyone actually wanted it.

*Lesson:* find a small group of people with a real problem and win them over one at a time.

**Watch:** [How to Talk to Users (Eric Migicovsky, Y Combinator)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MT4Ig2uqjTc)
(~32 min). A practical script for interviewing users that draws on *The Mom Test*, plus the 40%
check for product-market fit.

## Exercise

Your trio from Contract Negotiations (the Builder, the Connector and the Part-Timer) has
built **ShiftSwap**, an app that lets part-time hospitality staff swap shifts without
having to go through a 50-message WhatsApp group.

1. Write out the problem in one sentence. Whose problem is it and how are they dealing
   with it right now?
2. Write three *Mom Test* questions, and one bad question you would've asked before
   reading this.
3. Who's the user and who's the buyer here, the staff member or the restaurant manager?
4. Pick a pricing model and a price and justify it in two sentences.
5. Pick your beachhead (cafés in Limerick city, for example) and list how you'd get your
   first ten customers. What's *your* version of the Collison installation?
6. Try your questions in the **Customer Interview Simulator** below. How much real
   evidence did you get, and how many false positives?

Compare your answers with a partner and see whose beachhead is narrower. Usually the
narrower one wins.

## Interactive Element (optional)

### Customer Interview Simulator

The reader interviews "Gráinne", who manages a café in Limerick, about the ShiftSwap idea
and learns how to tell real evidence apart from people just being polite.

**1. Interview:** 6 rounds. Each round, tap one of three questions (one good, two bad).

- *Good (past, specific):* "Tell me about the last time a shift fell through." → reveals a
  fact (e.g. "Last Saturday, I spent an hour ringing staff and closed early.") → **+1 Evidence**
- *Bad (hypothetical):* "Would you use an app for shift swaps?" → "Ah yeah, sounds handy!"
  → **+1 False Positive**
- *Bad (pitching):* "Our app uses AI to match shifts. Cool, right?" → "Very clever!"
  → **+1 False Positive**

**2. The ask:** final round, choose how to close.

- "Let me know if you're interested." → nothing happens
- "Would you pay for this someday?" → "Maybe!" (worthless)
- "Can I set it up for your team on Friday, free for a month, then €30/month?" → real
  commitment → **Evidence ×2**

**Result:** Evidence vs. False Positives bar, a verdict (*Validated / Inconclusive / Fooled
by compliments*), and the questions to rephrase. Ending line: *"Stripe didn't send a link.
They asked for your laptop."*

**Accessibility:** keyboard-friendly cards; results stated in text, not just colour. Reuse
the card style from the Founders' Agreement Stress Test (Contract Negotiations topic).
