## Pre-reading Questions

Before you start, jot down a quick answer to each one. You'll come back to them at the end.

1. If you started a company with two friends tomorrow, how would you split the ownership,
   and what would you do if one of them stopped turning up after six months?
2. Think of a product you pay for. Why do you pay for it, and what would make you stop?
3. Would you rather own 100% of a company worth €100,000 or 20% of one worth €10 million?
   What would you have to give up to get the second one?
4. Who should a founder answer to? Investors, employees, customers, or nobody?
5. Where do you think the line is between "selling the vision" and lying to investors?

## Anecdotes

**The co-founder who got diluted (Facebook, 2005).** Eduardo Saverin put up the early money
for Facebook and held a large stake. When the company was restructured and new shares were
issued to bring in investors and staff, Saverin's share was diluted from roughly a third of the
company to a tiny fraction. He sued, and the case was settled with his co-founder title
restored. The issue wasn't the dilution itself (every funded startup dilutes) but that
nothing written down set out what each founder had agreed to. *Links to: Contract
Negotiations, Finance and Valuation.*

**The video that sold the product before it worked (Dropbox, 2007).** Syncing files
between computers was a hard thing to build, and Drew Houston didn't want to spend years on
it if nobody wanted it. So he made a short demo video showing how Dropbox would work and
posted it online. The beta waiting list went from about 5,000 to 75,000 sign-ups overnight.
That was the proof of demand he needed before the product was finished. *Links to:
Commercialisation.*

**The board that didn't ask questions (Theranos, 2003–2018).** Elizabeth Holmes raised
hundreds of millions of dollars by promising blood tests from a single finger-prick. Her board
was full of famous names from politics and the military but had almost no medical or
lab-science experts, and Holmes held enough voting control that they couldn't really overrule
her. The technology never worked as claimed. In 2022 she was convicted of defrauding
investors and sentenced to more than 11 years in prison. *Links to: Corruption &
Governance, Finance and Valuation.*

## Reflection Questions

Answer these once you've finished all four topics.

1. Go back to your pre-reading split for the three friends. Would you change it now? What
   would you add about vesting, roles and IP, and why?
2. Choose one company from this path (Snapchat, Stripe, Quibi, PayPal, Facebook, Dropbox or
   Theranos). Which founder decision mattered most to how things turned out, and which topic
   explains it best?
3. Your 100% of €100,000 vs. 20% of €10 million answer: has it changed? What would need to
   be true about the business for taking investment to be the right call?
4. Commercialisation says "start charging early" and Finance says "watch your burn". How do
   these two ideas back each other up?
5. Saverin, Reggie Brown and the Theranos board all show what happens when the rules aren't
   written down or enforced. Write three rules you'd want in place on day one of your own
   company, and say who would hold you to them.
6. Which topic changed your mind the most, and what did you believe before?
